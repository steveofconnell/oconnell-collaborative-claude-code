# Cross-Disciplinary Literature Search

Applies to: any search for prior work, novelty check, related-work section, funding
narrative, or claim that something has not been done — and with particular force to work
that crosses, or should cross, a disciplinary boundary. Always-on.

Origin, and the day this cost: `casebook/incidents.md` → literature-search.

## 1. Search in the target field's vocabulary, not the project's

**Before concluding a question is open, ask which other field would ask it, and in what
words.** A literature is invisible if it uses different terms for the same thing, and the
search terms that feel natural are exactly the ones that field does not use.

The economics vocabulary and the history vocabulary for the same object rarely overlap.
Neither overlaps with the sociology one. Run the search once per discipline, in that
discipline's terms, and treat a null result in your own vocabulary as uninformative about
anyone else's.

## 2. A zero word-count in one source proves only that that author avoids the term

Never read the absence of a term from one book, article or corpus as evidence that the
concept is unclaimed in the field. This inference is tempting because it is cheap and
checkable, and it is wrong: it measures one author's idiom.

The correct inference from a zero count is "this author does not use this word." The
question of whether the field has the concept is a separate search, and it is the one that
matters.

## 3. Work the nearest neighbour's bibliography before running a database search

The single highest-yield source is the reference list of the closest existing work. It is
pre-filtered by someone who spent years in the material, it names the sub-literatures by
their own names, and it surfaces the terms to search with — which fixes rule 1 as a side
effect. Extract it, classify it, and cross-check it against what is already held before
opening a database.

## 4. Search dissertation repositories

Unpublished and recently-published dissertations are a large share of active work on any
under-studied topic, they are frequently open access, and they are absent from the search
paths that find journal articles and books. A field can look empty because its current work
has not reached print.

## 5. When claiming a field is thin, name its monographs

A thin literature is a legitimate and often correct claim. **Assert it by enumerating the
field's books and saying in a clause what each does.** Asserting it while citing two
memoirs and an ethnography reads as not having looked, and the reader who knows the field
stops reading there.

Same claim, opposite impression, and the difference is one paragraph. This applies to
funding narratives, related-work sections, book proposals and job talks.

## 6. Look for the field's own stocktaking before writing your own gap statement

An under-studied field periodically takes stock of itself: a review essay, a roundtable on
a recent book, a special issue, a historiographical essay, a society presidential address.
**These name the field's gaps explicitly, in its own words, by the people whose opinion of
the gap counts.**

Find that document before asserting a gap yourself. It is cheaper than inferring one, it is
more authoritative, and it converts the claim from a novelty assertion into a request the
field has published. "This is new" invites an argument about whether it is new. "The
literature has asked for exactly this, here is where and by whom" does not.

Where to look: the field's review journal, roundtables and book symposia, special issues,
and anything titled as a state of the field.

## 7. Report the search, including what it did not cover

A literature search that names its vocabulary, its disciplines and its databases can be
audited and repeated. One reported as a conclusion cannot. When a sweep is bounded — one
field, one decade, one language — say so, because a silent bound reads as coverage.
